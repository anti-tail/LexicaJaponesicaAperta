1. About this page

Kätö nö Sıma is a tool for searching early texts of the Japonesian languages together, built as part of the comparative lexical database Lexica Japonesica. The full, local version also searches other materials in the same interface, but some of them cannot be published for copyright reasons, so this public version contains only the Old Japanese data of ONCOJ. The query languages, tag look-up and the display of structural types and paradigms are shared across all materials.

The name Kätö nö Sıma is a reconstruction under the eight-vowel hypothesis for Proto-Japonesian proposed by Shiori Shiratori: *kätö ‘word’, *nö ‘GEN’, *s[ı]ma ‘island’, meaning “island of language”.

We call the language family Japonesian rather than Japonic. The name Japonic gives the impression that its members are not on an equal footing, and it has not been well received, mainly by people in the Ryukyu Arc.

2. What is ONCOJ?

ONCOJ, the Oxford-NINJAL Corpus of Old Japanese, is a corpus of Old Japanese built jointly by the University of Oxford and the National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics (NINJAL). It aims to make all Old Japanese texts available as a lemmatized, parsed and annotated digital corpus. The project started in 2011 and was called the Oxford Corpus of Old Japanese (OCOJ) until 2017.

  • The current version contains all Old Japanese poetry, about 90,000 words; prose texts are not yet included.
  • Poems are given in original script and phonemic transcription, and each word is annotated for lemma, mode of writing (phonographic or logographic), morphology, constituency and grammatical function.
  • A dictionary with English glosses is linked to the lemmas by ID.
  • The ONCOJ website offers tree search and downloads; the annotation is published under CC BY 4.0.

3. Reading the transcription (Frellesvig–Whitman notation)

ONCOJ gives each poem in its original script (man'yōgana and logograms) side by side with a phonemic romanization. The romanization follows the notation of Frellesvig and Whitman (F&W), which writes the kō-rui / otsu-rui distinctions of Old Japanese (the so-called jōdai tokushu kanazukai) by inserting w or y between consonant and vowel.

Note which side gets the extra letter: otsu for i (kwi = ki₂), kō for e (kye = ke₁) and kō for o (kwo = ko₁). Plain ki ke ko are therefore ki₁, ke₂, ko₂ (or syllables without the distinction).

The mwo / mo distinction is found only in the Kojiki songs (37 tokens of mwo in this corpus).

Vowel-initial syllables and other consonants

  • Bare vowels are distinct from y- and w-syllables: i / wi, e / ye / we, o / wo. Here wi ye wo are syllables with the glides w/y, not kō/otsu markings.
  • The h-row of later Japanese is p (pana ‘flower’); voiced obstruents are b d g z; the others are k s t n m y r w.
  • w and y are a notational convention for writing the distinctions in ASCII, not necessarily a claim about pronunciation; the phonetic values of the kō/otsu vowels are debated.
  • In the lexicon, bound morphemes are written with hyphens (-ki-, the past auxiliary), and each lemma has an ID such as L050083.

4. ONCOJ and Chūnagon (Corpus of Historical Japanese)

Besides ONCOJ, Old Japanese can be searched in the Nara period series of NINJAL's Corpus of Historical Japanese (CHJ) through the search application Chūnagon. Both involve NINJAL, but they are built differently and are good at different things.

Rule of thumb
· Questions about structure (kakari-musubi, relative clauses, word order), searching by form including kō/otsu, or covering all Old Japanese poetry including the chronicle songs → ONCOJ
· Comparison with later periods on the same criteria, the prose of senmyō and norito, or vocabulary searches on a reading text → Chūnagon (CHJ)

The same poem may be read (kun) or segmented differently in the two corpora. The ONCOJ tree view links to the corresponding CHJ Nara-period text.

5. How this search page handles the data

  • The data come from the ONCOJ psd (tree) files. Lemma-ID nodes are folded into their POS nodes and writing nodes (PHON, LOG …) become word attributes; the annotation itself is unchanged. See “Tags & structure” → “Data model” on the search page.
  • “ignore kō/otsu” removes the w/y (kwo→ko, pwi→pi, kye→ke) before matching, in both the data and the query; “ignore voicing” treats g/k z/s d/t b/p as the same.
  • In “Words” mode Old Japanese can also be typed in kana (ヒとのコ). Katakana is read as kō or as a syllable without the distinction, hiragana as otsu, and dakuten marks voicing: ヒとのコ = pitonokwo, こひ = kopwi. Kana cannot distinguish e from ye, so エ matches both. Hiragana へ looks like katakana ヘ, so it is displayed in the search box as 倍, the source character of the hentaigana.
  • Kakyō hyōshiki is not included in this version.
